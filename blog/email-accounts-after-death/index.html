How to Close a Deceased Person's Email Accounts

By Departed Digital · 8 min read · Updated September 2026

Email looks like just another account to close. It is not. The inbox is the master key to everything else the person used: bank logins, utility bills, subscriptions, and social media accounts all send their password resets and confirmation messages there. Close it first and you can lock the family out of every other closure; leave it unattended and it becomes the single most valuable target for fraud against the estate.

So this guide starts with the one rule that matters more than any provider's process, then walks through what Gmail, Microsoft, and Yahoo each actually allow, and ends with a trap specific to email that catches out UK families in particular.

The Rule: Close Email Last

Almost every other account closure produces an email: a confirmation link, a verification code, a reply from a support team. If the inbox is already gone, those messages bounce and closures stall. The right order is:

1
Secure the inbox, don't close it

If the family has legitimate access on the person's own device, leave it logged in and untouched. If not, do nothing yet; simply make sure nobody else can get in (the person's unlocked phone or computer should not be passed around or sold).

2
Use it as a map

The inbox is the most complete record of the person's digital life that exists. Bank statements, subscription receipts, and account notices tell you exactly which companies need to hear the news. Our account checklist pairs well with an hour spent reading the inbox's senders.

3
Close everything else, then the email

Once the social media accounts, subscriptions, and services are dealt with and the estate's practical matters are settled, the inbox has done its job and can be closed.

Gmail and Google Accounts

A Gmail address is a full Google account, so closing it also erases Google Photos, Drive, and YouTube. Google has the most developed process of the three providers: a dedicated deceased user request form, no court order needed for closure, and a two-track system where families can request closure and, separately, request data in limited cases.

Because the stakes are wider than email, we cover Google in its own guide: how to close a deceased person's Google account, including the Inactive Account Manager and exactly what closure destroys.

Microsoft: Outlook, Hotmail, and Live

Microsoft covers outlook.com, hotmail.com, live.com, and msn.com addresses, plus OneDrive storage under the same login.

What families can do: request that the account be closed, with a death certificate and proof that you are a close relative or the estate's representative. Microsoft's support page for bereaved families ("Accessing Outlook.com, OneDrive and other Microsoft services when someone has died") is the starting point, and responses typically take a few weeks.

What families cannot do: get into the account or receive its contents. Microsoft discontinued its old next-of-kin release process; today it requires a valid court order before it will even consider releasing a deceased person's emails or files. For most families that makes the contents effectively unreachable, so if there is anything precious in a OneDrive or an Outlook inbox, the person's own logged-in devices are the realistic route to it.

The quiet deadline: Microsoft's inactivity rules can permanently remove Outlook messages and OneDrive files after as little as a year without a sign-in, and the account itself expires after two years. If content matters, act well before those anniversaries.

Yahoo and AOL

Yahoo and AOL sit under the same parent company and behave the same way.

What families can do: request closure of the account by providing a death certificate through the "options available when an account owner passes away" process in Yahoo's help pages. Closure stops the account being a fraud target and ends the mail build-up.

What families cannot do: obtain passwords or read the mail. Yahoo is the strictest of the three: it treats the account as private even after death, and releasing content requires a court order that satisfies its legal team. This position survived a famous US court fight, and it has not softened. Note also that Yahoo's US help process expects US-issued documents for US accounts; UK and other international accounts go through the regional versions of the same help pages, so start from the help site that matches the person's country.

The ISP Email Trap

Here is the one that catches UK families. Addresses ending in @btinternet.com, @sky.com, @virginmedia.com, or @talktalk.net are not standalone email accounts; they are features of a broadband contract. When the household cancels the broadband after a death, which is often one of the first practical jobs done, the email address is scheduled for deletion with it, sometimes after a short grace period, sometimes almost immediately.

Families lose access to the very inbox that every bank, insurer, and utility company is sending confirmations to, mid-way through settling the estate.

Before cancelling any broadband or phone contract, check whether the person's main email address belongs to that provider. If it does, spend a week forwarding what matters, updating the estate's key contacts to a different address, and saving important messages, before the contract is touched. Some providers will keep the mailbox alive briefly for a fee or on request; none of them keep it forever.

Why Closing Email Matters at All

It can feel harmless to leave an inbox unattended, and unlike social media, it is invisible to friends and family. But an abandoned email account is the most common way deceased people become fraud victims: whoever controls the inbox can reset passwords for everything attached to it. Recyclable addresses make it worse, because some providers eventually release dormant addresses for re-registration. Closing the account, at the right time, permanently removes that risk.

The Documents You Will Need

All three providers want the same core evidence: the death certificate, plus proof of your authority, which is a grant of probate or letters of administration in the UK, letters testamentary in the US, or evidence of next-of-kin relationship where no formal estate process exists.

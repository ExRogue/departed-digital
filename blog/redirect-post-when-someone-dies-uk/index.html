How to Redirect or Stop Post When Someone Dies in the UK

By Steven Kong · 7 min read · Updated September 2026

For months after a death, the post keeps coming. Bank statements, renewal reminders, catalogues, sometimes a birthday offer from a company that does not know. Each one lands on the doormat with their name on it, and many families find it one of the hardest parts of the first few weeks.

You can stop most of it, and have the rest sent to you wherever you are. Before you do, though, it helps to know that their post is also the most complete list you will ever get of the accounts, contracts and subscriptions they had. This guide covers both: how to get the post under control, and how to make use of it.

In short: use Tell Us Once for the government post. Ask Royal Mail to redirect everything else to you, which you do by post or at a Post Office rather than online. Register them with the Bereavement Register to stop the marketing. For anything that matters, contact the sender directly, because sending a letter back does not tell them about the death.

Read It Before You Stop It

Every letter names an organisation that had a relationship with them, which is exactly the list the person sorting out their affairs needs. For the first few weeks, keep all of it in one box, including the envelopes that look like junk. Then go through it looking for:

  • Bank, building society and credit card statements. These also show every direct debit and card payment. Look for names like Apple, Google, Amazon, PayPal, Netflix and Spotify, which point to online accounts that never send paper.
  • Renewal notices for insurance, breakdown cover, antivirus software, website domains, memberships and magazines.
  • Letters from the mobile network and broadband provider. Keep the mobile number active for now, because many online accounts send their security codes to it.
  • Pension, investment and savings statements, including accounts the family did not know about.

If you are the executor, or you are the person dealing with their affairs, going through their post is a normal part of that job. If someone else is the executor, agree between you who opens it, so nothing is missed or handled twice.

Our guide to finding out which online accounts someone had covers the other places to look, and the checklist of digital accounts gives you somewhere to write them down.

Start With Tell Us Once

When you register the death in England, Scotland or Wales, the registrar will offer you Tell Us Once. It reports the death to most government organisations in one go: HM Revenue and Customs, the Department for Work and Pensions, the Passport Office, the DVLA, the local council, Veterans UK, Social Security Scotland and public sector pension schemes. Once they know, their letters stop.

The registrar either completes it with you there and then, or gives you a reference number to use online or by phone. The reference only lasts 28 days, so use it early.

Tell Us Once does not reach private organisations. Banks, mortgage lenders, insurers, energy and water companies, employers, private pension schemes and landlords all need telling separately. It is also not available if the person lived in Northern Ireland or lived abroad permanently. In Northern Ireland, nidirect.gov.uk explains who to tell instead.

Redirect Their Post With Royal Mail

If their home will be empty, or you are dealing with everything from somewhere else, Royal Mail can send the post addressed to them to your address instead. Letters stop piling up behind the door of an empty house, which Royal Mail also points out reduces the risk of identity fraud, and you see every one of them.

1
Use the Special Circumstances form

Redirection for someone who has died uses Royal Mail's Special Circumstances application form, which you can download from royalmail.com/redirection. Unlike an ordinary house move, it cannot be set up online. Royal Mail says that for security reasons you need to apply by post or at a Post Office branch.

2
Show that you are dealing with their affairs

Royal Mail asks to see documents showing you are entitled to act for them, such as the death certificate and the grant of probate, letters of administration, or the will that names you as executor. The form sets out exactly which documents it accepts, so check it before you post it or go to the counter.

3
Choose how long

Redirection runs for three, six or twelve months at a time, and Royal Mail invites you to renew a few weeks before it ends. For someone who has died it can run for up to four years in total, which is usually long enough to finish dealing with the estate. At the time of writing, in September 2026, Royal Mail's redirection prices start at £45 for three months.

If they lived in a care home or hospital: Royal Mail cannot redirect post from nursing homes, hospitals or other buildings where many people share one letterbox. Ask the home to pass on anything that arrives, and give the important senders your own address when you tell them about the death.

A few things will still arrive at the old address once a redirection starts, because Royal Mail does not redirect them: post addressed "To the Occupier", and parcels from couriers and other delivery companies. Royal Mail also says it may have to tell the authorities about redirection requests for mail relating to government benefits, which is one more reason to do Tell Us Once first.

Stop the Marketing Post

Catalogues and offers addressed to someone who has died are the post families tend to find hardest, and they are the easiest to stop.

1
Register them with the Bereavement Register

The Bereavement Register is free for families, because the direct marketing companies pay to use it. You add their details online at thebereavementregister.org.uk, and companies that check the register remove them from their mailing lists. The register says you should see a significant reduction within six weeks, and virtually none within six months.

2
Add the Mailing Preference Service

Royal Mail suggests also registering with the Mailing Preference Service at mpsonline.org.uk, to reduce direct marketing sent in the person's name. It is free too.

Neither service stops leaflets addressed "To the Occupier", unaddressed mail or post from overseas. Neither stops bills and statements either, which only end when you tell the organisation.

What to Do With a Letter Addressed to Them

Most letters fall into one of two groups, and each needs something different.

From an organisation they had a relationship with, such as a bank, energy supplier, phone network, insurer or subscription: contact the organisation, tell them about the death and ask what they need. Most will want a copy of the death certificate and the executor's details. Sending the letter back does not do this job for you. Royal Mail delivers to addresses rather than names, so a returned envelope only tells the sender the address did not work.

Marketing from companies they never really dealt with: add them to the Bereavement Register, then send back anything that keeps arriving. Royal Mail's advice is to cross out the address, write "Not known at this address" or "No longer lives here" on the envelope, and put it in a postbox. You do not need a stamp.

If you live at the same address, nothing changes about the delivery itself. Their post keeps coming to the house, and it tails off as each organisation is told.

The Online Accounts the Post Points To

Paper only tells you about the organisations that still send paper. Email, social media, cloud storage and most subscriptions show up on a bank statement at best, and each has its own process after a death.

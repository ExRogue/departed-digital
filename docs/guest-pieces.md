## Piece one: What to do with a loved one's phone (about 600 words)

Their phone is the most useful thing they left, and the easiest to ruin. It holds the photos, the messages and the last voicemail, and it receives the security codes that unlock their other accounts. One well meaning relative resetting it "so it can be sold" can lose all of that in a minute.

Keep it charged and switched on. Turn notifications off if they are painful, but leave the phone powered, because other accounts will send it text messages to prove it is you. Keep the mobile number going for a few months for the same reason; every UK network will keep a line open when you explain, and none of them may charge an early termination fee because the customer has died.

Do not reset it. Not yet. On an iPhone or an Android phone a reset deletes everything that is not backed up and locks the phone to the person's account, so nobody can use it anyway.

If you know the passcode, do the useful things first, before you contact any company. Back the phone up to a computer. Copy the photos somewhere safe. Export the WhatsApp conversations you want to keep, because WhatsApp deletes an account after 120 days without use. Open the email and search it for "renewal" and "your account" to find the accounts nobody knew about. Record the voicemail greeting by ringing the number from another phone. Knowing the passcode does not make you the account holder, so closing and memorialising still go through each company's own process, but everything above is yours to do today.

If you do not know the passcode, the phone itself is out of reach. Neither Apple nor Google will unlock a phone for a family, whatever documents you bring, and guessing can wipe it. But most of what is on the phone is also somewhere else. Photos are usually in iCloud or Google Photos, and a laptop, a tablet or a relative's shared album often holds copies. If the person set up Apple's Legacy Contact or Google's Inactive Account Manager, the named person can get in with a death certificate. If not, both companies require a court order for the contents, which is a decision to make with a solicitor, and often not worth it once the family finds where else the photos live.

When is it safe to wipe? Only after every account that used the phone for codes has been closed or moved, the photos are saved elsewhere, and the person's Apple or Google account has been removed from the device with proof of purchase. Then it can be sold, donated or handed down.

## Piece two: What happens to a phone contract when someone dies (about 600 words)

A phone contract does not end when the person does. The direct debit continues, any handset finance continues, and the network has no idea anything has changed until someone tells it. Families regularly find contracts still running months after a funeral, sometimes several.

The rules are kinder than people expect. Ofcom, which regulates UK phone and broadband providers, says a provider should close the account of someone who has died when asked, and that the family should not have to pay an early termination charge for ending the contract early. Charges already owed can in principle be recovered from the estate, but providers often waive those as well. The one exception is a phone bought on a separate loan: that balance is a debt of the estate even though the airtime contract closes without penalty, so ask the provider to confirm in writing which parts have been waived.

Every provider has a bereavement route. Some have a dedicated team and phone line, some an online form, some an email address. All of them ask for roughly the same things: the account or phone number, the account holder's name and address, the death certificate or its registration number, your own name and relationship, and a decision about what you want done. The options are usually to close the account, to transfer it into another family member's name after a credit check, or to keep the number by moving it to another contract with a PAC code.

Before you cancel, think about three things. First, keep the SIM working for a few months: the person's email, bank and social media accounts send security codes to that number, and some become unreachable once it is gone. Second, decide whether anyone wants to keep the number, and save the voicemail greeting by ringing it from another phone. Third, if the contract includes broadband, remember the email address that came with it. BT, Sky, TalkTalk and Plusnet addresses close with the account, and some people used them for everything, so save what matters first.

If something goes wrong, a bill or a fee after closure, or weeks of silence, make a formal complaint to the provider quoting Ofcom's guidance. If it is not resolved within eight weeks, or the provider issues a deadlock letter, the Communications Ombudsman or CISAS will look at it for free.

The contract is usually the easy part. The harder part is what the phone was signed in to: the Apple or Google account, the email, the social media. Free step by step guides for each of those, and every UK provider's bereavement contact in one place, are at departed.digital/blog.
